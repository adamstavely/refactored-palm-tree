<div class="pg-eye">02 — Framework Primer</div>
<div class="pg-title">Team Topologies</div>
<p class="pg-desc">A framework for organizing software delivery teams for fast flow. Developed by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais (2019), it provides a structured approach to team design that treats cognitive load as the primary constraint on delivery speed and quality.</p>
<div class="tt-intro">
<div class="tt-intro-hd">What Is Team Topologies?</div>
<p>Team Topologies argues that the way an organization structures its teams determines its ability to deliver software quickly and sustainably. Org structure shapes communication patterns — and communication patterns shape software architecture. This is Conway's Law: systems reflect the communication structures of the organizations that build them. Getting team design wrong creates invisible coupling, cognitive overload, and delivery friction that no amount of tooling or process can fix.</p>
<p>The framework defines four team types and three interaction modes. Every team should have a clear type, and every team-to-team relationship should operate in a defined interaction mode. Ambiguity in either is a predictable source of delivery failure. The entire architecture of the framework exists to enforce one boundary: <strong style="color:var(--text);">cognitive load</strong>. Stream-aligned teams should never carry cognitive load that belongs to a platform, subsystem, or enabling function.</p>
<div class="tt-principles">
<div class="tt-principle">
<div class="tt-principle-hd">Conway's Law</div>
<p>Software architecture mirrors the communication structure of the teams that build it. Deliberately design teams to produce the architecture you want — not the other way around.</p>
</div>
<div class="tt-principle">
<div class="tt-principle-hd">Cognitive Load as Constraint</div>
<p>Every team has a finite capacity for complexity. Team type and interaction mode bound what each team is responsible for knowing and doing. Overloaded teams slow down and produce lower quality work.</p>
</div>
<div class="tt-principle">
<div class="tt-principle-hd">Team-First Thinking</div>
<p>The team — not the individual — is the unit of delivery. Teams should be stable, long-lived, and autonomous within their bounded domain. Frequent re-orgs destroy delivery throughput.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<hr class="pg-divider">
<div class="pg-sub-eye">The Four Team Types</div>
<div class="tt-types-grid">
<div class="tt-type" style="--tc:var(--teal);--tbg:var(--teal-bg);">
<div class="tt-type-letter">SA</div>
<div class="tt-type-badge">Stream-Aligned</div>
<h3>Aligned to a Flow of Work</h3>
<p>The primary and most common team type. A stream-aligned team is aligned to a single, valuable stream of work — a product, a service, a user journey, or a business domain. It owns end-to-end delivery within its stream: building, running, and evolving its part of the system.</p>
<p>Stream-aligned teams deliver value independently, without waiting on other teams for routine work. They consume platform services and enabling support — they do not build them. This is the fundamental cognitive load boundary the rest of the framework protects.</p>
<div class="tt-signals-hd">Healthy signals</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Owns and delivers against a product backlog without external gate approvals for routine work</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Cognitive load bounded by domain scope, not technology breadth</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Can deploy independently and frequently without coordination overhead</div>
<div class="tt-applies"><strong>In this office:</strong> Sustain &amp; Sunset domain teams, Mission-Critical Ops, Internal Tools &amp; Back-Office, THEMIS Product Team.</div>
</div>
<div class="tt-type" style="--tc:var(--sky);--tbg:var(--sky-bg);">
<div class="tt-type-letter">PT</div>
<div class="tt-type-badge">Platform</div>
<h3>Accelerates Other Teams</h3>
<p>Platform teams build and maintain the internal infrastructure, tooling, and services that stream-aligned teams use to deliver faster and with less cognitive load. A platform team's product is the developer experience — the golden paths, self-service APIs, and paved roads that make other teams more productive.</p>
<p>Platform teams interact with stream-aligned teams almost exclusively in X-as-a-Service mode. If a stream-aligned team must collaborate extensively with the platform team to get routine work done, the platform has failed. The goal is frictionless self-service consumption.</p>
<div class="tt-signals-hd">Healthy signals</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Stream-aligned teams can consume platform services without tickets or support wait times</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Platform measures success by adoption rate and time-to-onboard, not just uptime</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Platform is treated as an internal product with versioned APIs and published documentation</div>
<div class="tt-applies"><strong>In this office:</strong> Provided externally by the Platform &amp; Enablement office (DevSecOps, QA, infrastructure, enterprise architecture). Not internally staffed within this SDO.</div>
</div>
<div class="tt-type" style="--tc:var(--violet);--tbg:var(--violet-bg);">
<div class="tt-type-letter">CS</div>
<div class="tt-type-badge">Complicated Subsystem</div>
<h3>Deep Specialist Expertise</h3>
<p>Complicated subsystem teams own components that require specialist knowledge too deep or risky for stream-aligned teams to build and maintain. The complexity is not just high — it is the kind that genuinely requires dedicated experts to operate safely. These teams publish stable contracts to consuming teams.</p>
<p>Unlike platform teams, complicated subsystem teams do not serve all teams broadly — they serve specific consumers. They do not own feature delivery; they own a complex technical component that enables delivery.</p>
<div class="tt-signals-hd">Healthy signals</div>
<div class="tt-signal">The subsystem requires skills that cannot be reasonably distributed across stream-aligned teams</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Consumers interact via stable, versioned contracts — not ad-hoc collaboration requests</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Team maintains SDK, docs, and sandbox environments for consumer onboarding</div>
<div class="tt-applies"><strong>In this office:</strong> AI Platform &amp; MLOps CoE, Auth &amp; Identity Services, API &amp; Integration Layer, Shared Backend Services.</div>
</div>
<div class="tt-type" style="--tc:var(--green);--tbg:var(--green-bg);">
<div class="tt-type-letter">EN</div>
<div class="tt-type-badge">Enabling</div>
<h3>Builds Capability in Others</h3>
<p>Enabling teams help stream-aligned teams acquire capabilities they don't currently have. Critically, the goal is to grow capability in served teams — not create permanent dependency. Engagement is typically time-limited: embed, upskill, leave a pattern behind, step back.</p>
<p>An enabling team that becomes a permanent fixture has failed. An enabling team that becomes a bottleneck has reverted to traditional shared services. Success is measured by reducing the need for enabling support over time — not by growing the enabling team's own headcount.</p>
<div class="tt-signals-hd">Healthy signals</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Stream-aligned teams need less enabling support over time, not more</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Engagements are time-bounded and leave documented patterns, not dependencies</div>
<div class="tt-signal">Enabling team tracks capability growth in other teams as its primary metric</div>
<div class="tt-applies"><strong>In this office:</strong> HCD &amp; UX Design, User Training. User Support is operationally ongoing but lives within the enabling division to close the feedback loop.</div>
</div>
</div>
<hr class="pg-divider">
<div class="pg-sub-eye">The Three Interaction Modes</div>
<div class="im-grid">
<div class="im-card" style="--ic:var(--teal);">
<div class="im-label">X-as-a-Service</div>
<div class="im-body">
<h4>Consume without collaboration</h4>
<p>One team consumes a service, API, or tool provided by another with minimal interaction. The consuming team uses the interface — it doesn't need to understand implementation. The providing team maintains a stable, well-documented contract. This is the highest-bandwidth mode: it scales without growing the provider team.</p>
<div class="im-when">Use when the interface is stable and well-documented and consuming teams can self-serve. If teams regularly need to open tickets or wait for provider help to do routine work, the XaaS boundary has broken down.</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="im-card" style="--ic:var(--violet);">
<div class="im-label">Collaboration</div>
<div class="im-body">
<h4>Work together on shared complexity</h4>
<p>Two teams work closely together for a defined period to discover or solve something neither could do alone. Collaboration is high-bandwidth, high-value, and deliberately time-limited. It should produce a clearer interface, a new capability, or a new XaaS boundary — not become permanent joint ownership.</p>
<div class="im-when">Use when there is genuine uncertainty about the right approach, new technology is being adopted, or a new service boundary needs to be discovered through working together. Not for routine delivery.</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="im-card" style="--ic:var(--green);">
<div class="im-label">Facilitation</div>
<div class="im-body">
<h4>Unblock and upskill</h4>
<p>An enabling team works alongside a stream-aligned team to help them overcome a capability gap. The enabling team is not doing the work for them — it is building capability so the stream-aligned team can do it themselves. Facilitation ends when the gap is closed.</p>
<div class="im-when">Use when a stream-aligned team lacks a skill or practice needed for delivery. The enabling team detects the need, engages for a defined period, and exits with a documented pattern in the stream-aligned team's hands.</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="tt-note">
<strong>Platform Teams in This Office:</strong> Platform teams are the fourth Team Topologies type but are not directly staffed within the Software Development Office. The external Platform &amp; Enablement office acts as the platform team for this org — providing CI/CD, infrastructure, DevSecOps, QA frameworks, and enterprise architecture as a service. All five SDO divisions consume that office in X-as-a-Service mode. This is a deliberate boundary: the SDO focuses on application delivery; platform concerns live outside it.
</div>
</div>

<div class="pg-eye">04 — Operating Model</div>
<div class="pg-title">How the Office Runs</div>
<p class="pg-desc">Four operating model components govern how work enters the office, how teams run, how they interact, and how the workforce transitions from legacy to modern over the 6–18 month horizon.</p>
<div class="op-grid">
<div class="op-card">
<h3>Two-Lane Demand Management</h3>
<p class="op-desc">Portfolio &amp; Demand Management separates O&amp;M demand from new development. Without this separation, legacy work crowds out modernization. Both lanes are governed but prioritized independently.</p>
<div class="demand-lanes">
<div class="demand-lane" style="--lc:var(--amber);">
<div class="lane-hd">Lane 1 · O&amp;M</div>
<div class="lane-step">Incidents, change requests, and maintenance</div>
<div class="lane-step">Triaged by Portfolio &amp; Demand Mgmt</div>
<div class="lane-step">Routed to Sustain &amp; Sunset teams</div>
<div class="lane-step">SLA-governed; not competing with new dev</div>
</div>
<div class="demand-lane" style="--lc:var(--teal);">
<div class="lane-hd">Lane 2 · New Dev</div>
<div class="lane-step">New features, apps, and AI capabilities</div>
<div class="lane-step">Prioritized against a unified backlog</div>
<div class="lane-step">Routed to MAD, AIE, or Common Services</div>
<div class="lane-step">Capacity-governed; protected from O&amp;M bleed</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="op-card">
<h3>Workforce Transition Model</h3>
<p class="op-desc">The office is designed to shrink in one place and grow in others. The Director of Sustain &amp; Sunset owns active transition planning. Receiving divisions plan for capacity absorption.</p>
<div class="t-steps">
<div class="t-step"><div class="t-from">App reaches sunset</div><div class="t-arr">→</div><div class="t-to">Contractors wound down; FTE skill-match assessed</div></div>
<div class="t-step"><div class="t-from">FTE: modern dev skills</div><div class="t-arr">→</div><div class="t-to">Transition pathway to Modern App Development</div></div>
<div class="t-step"><div class="t-from">FTE: ML / AI skills</div><div class="t-arr">→</div><div class="t-to">Transition pathway to AI Engineering Division</div></div>
<div class="t-step"><div class="t-from">Domain fully sunset</div><div class="t-arr">→</div><div class="t-to">Domain team dissolved; sequencing managed with TD</div></div>

<div class="op-card sprint-card">
<h3>Standardized Sprint Cadence</h3>
<p class="op-desc">Every team in the office operates on the same 2-week sprint cycle with synchronized boundaries. This is a structural decision — not a convention — enabling cross-team coordination, predictable planning, and office-wide delivery visibility.</p>
<div class="sprint-pillars">
<div class="sprint-pillar">
<div class="sprint-pillar-hd">Sprint Length</div>
<p>2 weeks, fixed. No team operates a different cadence without Director and Technical Director approval.</p>
</div>
<div class="sprint-pillar">
<div class="sprint-pillar-hd">Synchronized Boundaries</div>
<p>All teams start and end on the same calendar day. Sprint 1 start date is established at office kickoff and governs all teams.</p>
</div>
<div class="sprint-pillar">
<div class="sprint-pillar-hd">Why It Matters</div>
<p>Shared sprint boundaries enable cross-team dependency planning, synchronized demos, and portfolio-level reporting without translation overhead.</p>
</div>
<div class="sprint-pillar">
<div class="sprint-pillar-hd">Office Ceremonies</div>
<p>Sprint Review: last Friday of each sprint. Leadership sync: first Monday of each sprint. PI Planning: every 5 sprints (~10 weeks).</p>

<div class="pg-eye">06 — Engineering Culture</div>
<div class="pg-title">Engineering Principles</div>
<p class="pg-desc">Six principles that govern how this office builds, operates, and grows. These are not aspirational values — they are behavioral commitments that define what it means to be an engineer in this office. Every engineering decision should be traceable to at least one of these principles.</p>
<div class="ep-charter">
<div class="ep-charter-text">
<h3>How to Use These Principles</h3>
<p>Engineering principles are most useful when they resolve ambiguity — when two reasonable paths exist and a team needs a shared standard to decide. These six principles are ordered by architectural priority, not importance. All six apply at all times. When principles appear to conflict, the mission context determines which takes precedence, and that decision should be documented.</p>
</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pills">
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:var(--teal);border-color:var(--teal);background:var(--teal-bg);">Security &amp; Resiliency</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:var(--green);border-color:var(--green);background:var(--green-bg);">Accountability</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:#84cc16;border-color:#84cc16;background:rgba(132,204,22,.1);">Human-Centered</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:var(--amber);border-color:var(--amber);background:var(--amber-bg);">Tradecraft</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:var(--orange);border-color:var(--orange);background:var(--orange-bg);">Trustworthy AI</div>
<div class="ep-charter-pill" style="color:#e879f9;border-color:#e879f9;background:rgba(232,121,249,.1);">Modularity</div>

<div class="ep-card-accent"></div>
<div class="ep-card-body">
<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 01</div>
<div class="ep-title">Build to Endure,<br>Engineer to Protect</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We treat security &amp; resiliency as a first principle; embedded in every decision, design, and deployment.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Embed zero trust, confidentiality, and availability into every layer of design, development, and deployment. Engineer for high availability, graceful degradation, and redundancy — not as features, but as structural requirements.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Continuously adapt to stay ahead of threats. Security posture is never static; threat modeling, dependency review, and adversarial thinking are standing activities, not periodic audits.</div>
</div>

<div class="ep-card" style="--ep-a:#059669;--ep-b:#0d9488;">
<div class="ep-card-accent"></div>
<div class="ep-card-body">
<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 02</div>
<div class="ep-title">Mission-Driven<br>Accountability</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We own our impact &amp; outcomes — technical, operational, and ethical.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Engineers are responsible for the mission impact, security, and operational behavior of their work. Ownership does not end at code merge — it extends to production behavior, downstream effects, and mission consequences.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Openly acknowledge mistakes through blameless postmortems and use telemetry to drive continuous learning, resilience, and improvement. A blame-free culture requires a truth-seeking culture — both must be present.</div>

<div class="ep-bg-num">2</div>

<div class="ep-card" style="--ep-a:#84cc16;--ep-b:#d97706;">

<div class="ep-card-body">
<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 03</div>
<div class="ep-title">Human-Centered Design<br>&amp; Engineering</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We build with and for the user.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Involve end users and domain experts early and often to ensure solutions align with real mission needs. User research and usability testing are engineering activities, not UX afterthoughts — they belong in the delivery process alongside architecture review and code review.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Prioritize intuitive, usable designs and continuously adapt based on user feedback and evolving requirements. A system that works correctly but cannot be used effectively has failed its mission.</div>

<div class="ep-bg-num">3</div>

<div class="ep-card" style="--ep-a:#d97706;--ep-b:#ea580c;">
<div class="ep-card-accent"></div>
<div class="ep-card-body">
<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 04</div>
<div class="ep-title">Tradecraft<br>Mastery</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We grow talent as deliberately as we build technology.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Promote a culture of lifelong learning and invest in ongoing mastery of secure development, AI/ML ethics, leadership, and core tradecraft skills. Engineers who stop learning produce systems that stop improving — skill development is a delivery requirement, not a benefit.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Encourage mentorship, knowledge sharing, and the development of sound technical and mission judgment. Expertise that lives only in individuals is a single point of failure. Documenting, pairing, and teaching are engineering obligations.</div>

<div class="ep-card" style="--ep-a:#ea580c;--ep-b:#e11d48;">

<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 05</div>
<div class="ep-title">Trustworthy<br>Autonomy</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We augment, not replace, human judgment.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Ensure all AI and automation are explainable, traceable, and always under meaningful human control — with built-in trust calibration and human-in-the-loop oversight. Automation that cannot be interrogated cannot be trusted, and automation that cannot be trusted cannot be fielded.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Proactively test, challenge, and validate autonomy through red teaming, adversarial exercises, and bias mitigation to maintain trust and mission alignment. Trustworthy systems are proven, not assumed.</div>

<div class="ep-num">Engineering Principle 06</div>
<div class="ep-title">Loosely Coupled,<br>Highly Aligned</div>
<div class="ep-tagline">We engineer capabilities that evolve with mission.</div>
<div class="ep-bullets">
<div class="ep-bullet">Use open standards, well-documented APIs, and modular components to enable agility, collaboration, and capability reuse across domains and missions. Every interface is a contract; contracts must be designed for extension, not just for today's consumer.</div>
<div class="ep-bullet">Design systems to avoid platform lock-in and adapt quickly to adversarial threats, shifting priorities, and emerging technologies. Technical flexibility is a mission requirement — the threat landscape, technology landscape, and mission requirements will all change; the architecture must be able to change with them.</div>

<div class="page" id="page-talent">
<div class="pg-eye">07 — Technical Talent</div>
<div class="pg-title">Technical Staff Structure<br>&amp; Career Framework</div>
<p class="pg-desc">The office organizes technical talent on a single principle: experts lead experts. From entry-level engineers to the Technical Director, every leadership role is held by someone with deep expertise in the domain they lead. This is not a preference — it is a structural requirement that shapes how the office hires, promotes, and evaluates its people.</p>
<div class="talent-philosophy">
<div class="talent-philosophy-hd">The Experts-Lead-Experts Paradigm</div>
<p>Conventional organizations separate management from expertise. As companies grow, they tend to install general managers above technical teams — people who can coordinate but cannot evaluate, judge, or improve the work beneath them. The result is predictable: technical decisions get slower, quality declines, and the best individual contributors leave because they can't learn from their managers.</p>
<p>This office takes a different position, one validated by Apple's functional organization model: the assumption is that it is easier to train an expert to manage well than to train a general manager to develop genuine technical expertise. Engineering leaders at every level — team lead, division director, deputy director, technical director — must be capable of engaging substantively with the work their teams produce. They must be able to push, probe, and evaluate decisions, not just track them. Leaders are chosen for domain mastery first; management capability is developed from that foundation.</p>
<p>Three behaviors define what experts-lead-experts looks like in practice. All three are expected of every engineering leader in this office, calibrated to their level and span of responsibility.</p>
<div class="exp-trio">
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Deep Expertise</div>
<p>Leaders possess genuine expertise in the domain they lead and can engage meaningfully with technical work at multiple levels of detail. They are recognized as authoritative within their specialty — not merely senior in title. Engineers choose this org because they can learn from the people above them.</p>
</div>
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Immersion in Details</div>
<p>Leaders know the details of their organization at least three levels deep. This is essential for effective cross-functional decision-making. Leaders who cannot engage with the details of their domain cannot evaluate trade-offs, detect failure, or earn the trust of their engineers.</p>
</div>
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Collaborative Debate</div>
<p>Leaders hold strong, well-grounded views and advocate for them — and are willing to change their minds when presented with better evidence. Effective functional organizations require leaders who can be simultaneously partisan and open-minded, separating how right a decision is from how hard it is to execute.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="pg-sub-eye" style="margin-bottom:12px;">Discretionary Leadership Model</div>
<div class="talent-philosophy" style="margin-bottom:28px;">
<p>As engineering leaders take on larger spans and broader responsibilities, they cannot own everything at the same level of detail. The Discretionary Leadership Model, adapted from Apple University, defines four modes of engagement that leaders use to allocate their attention across the portfolio of work they are responsible for.</p>
<div class="dlm-grid">
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-own">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">High Expertise · Deep Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Owning</div>
<p>Areas where the leader has deep expertise and is highly involved in the details. The leader sets direction, evaluates the work, and is accountable for the outcome. This is the core of what it means to be an expert leading experts.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-lrn">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">Low Expertise · Deep Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Learning</div>
<p>New domains the leader must grow into. Requires a genuine beginner's mindset — asking questions without pretending to have answers. Only critical new responsibilities should be in this quadrant; it is expensive and time-consuming.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-tch">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">High Expertise · Lower Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Teaching</div>
<p>Areas of deep expertise where the leader has developed others sufficiently to reduce direct involvement. The leader provides critique, feedback, and standards rather than owning the decisions. Requires experts to teach — general managers cannot do this.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-del">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">Lower Expertise · Lower Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Delegating</div>
<p>Areas where others have the requisite capability and the leader sets objectives, monitors progress, and holds teams accountable. This is closer to general management. Leaders in this office should spend the minority of their time here.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="page" id="page-culture">
<div class="pg-eye">07 — Organizational Culture</div>
<div class="pg-title">Building Culture<br>Deliberately</div>
<p class="pg-desc">Culture is not a values poster. It is the accumulated weight of what an organization signals, celebrates, repeats, and decides — especially under pressure. These four levers are how the SDO leadership team actively shapes and sustains the culture we want, rather than inheriting the culture we get by default.</p>
<div class="culture-intro">
<div class="culture-intro-hd">The Four Levers of Culture</div>
<p>Every organization has a culture — the question is whether it was designed or accumulated. Designed cultures are built through consistent, intentional use of four levers: the signals leaders send through their attention and behavior, the stories that circulate about what the organization values, the rituals that create shared identity and rhythm, and the decisions that reveal what the organization actually believes when trade-offs are forced.</p>
<p>For the SDO, these four levers must pull in the same direction: toward an engineering culture that is mission-driven, expert-led, human-centered, and psychologically safe. When they conflict — when a decision contradicts a stated value, or a story circulates that rewards the wrong behavior — the inconsistency is louder than any value statement. Leadership must maintain coherence across all four.</p>
</div>
<div class="culture-four">
<div class="culture-card" style="--cc:var(--blue);">
<div class="culture-card-head">
<div class="culture-card-num">Lever 01 — Signals &amp; Signs</div>
<div class="culture-card-title">What Leaders Pay<br>Attention To</div>
<div class="culture-card-def">Signs are the visible behaviors, artifacts, and patterns that signal what the organization truly values — regardless of what it says it values. Engineers watch what leaders notice, reward, correct, and ignore. Over time these observations become the real operating norms. Signs include: what gets celebrated in all-hands, whose work gets highlighted, which metrics leaders ask about first, and how leaders behave during incidents or failures.</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card-body">
<div class="culture-card-q">Signs we are actively building in the SDO</div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Engineering quality over delivery velocity.</strong> When leaders ask about code health, test coverage, and architectural decisions — not just sprint burndown — engineers learn that quality is real, not aspirational.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Recognition of invisible work.</strong> Documentation, mentoring, code review, and operational improvements are recognized publicly alongside feature delivery.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Director visibility in the work.</strong> Division directors are expected to engage with the technical details of their domains — their presence in design reviews and code discussions signals that expertise is expected at every level.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Sunset milestones treated as wins.</strong> Successfully retiring a legacy system is celebrated as a delivery achievement — not mourned as a loss of team identity.</div></div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card" style="--cc:var(--violet);">
<div class="culture-card-head">
<div class="culture-card-num">Lever 02 — Stories</div>
<div class="culture-card-title">What the Organization<br>Remembers &amp; Repeats</div>
<div class="culture-card-def">Stories are the narratives that travel through an organization — told in onboarding, all-hands, team meetings, and hallway conversations. They encode values more powerfully than any policy document because they are memorable, human, and specific. The stories an organization chooses to tell reveal what it actually believes. The stories it fails to tell — or actively suppresses — reveal what it is afraid to confront.</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card-body">
<div class="culture-card-q">Stories the SDO must actively tell</div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Blameless postmortem stories.</strong> Specific, named examples of incidents where honest root-cause analysis led to a better system — told publicly and credited to the team, not the individuals who "failed."</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Expertise-over-title stories.</strong> Engineers whose technical judgment overrode a senior decision — and were right — told to reinforce that expertise earns authority in this office, not seniority alone.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>User impact stories.</strong> Named examples of how a specific engineering decision — a UX improvement, a latency reduction, a data enrichment — changed an analyst's or operator's experience. Connects code to mission.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Modernization origin stories.</strong> The founding narrative of why this office exists — the cost of legacy, the mission gap it creates, and the commitment to close it — told repeatedly so engineers understand what they are part of.</div></div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card" style="--cc:var(--teal);">
<div class="culture-card-head">
<div class="culture-card-num">Lever 03 — Rituals</div>
<div class="culture-card-title">What the Organization<br>Does Repeatedly</div>
<div class="culture-card-def">Rituals are recurring practices that create shared identity, rhythm, and belonging. They differ from processes in that they carry symbolic weight beyond their functional purpose — a sprint retrospective is a process, but a blameless postmortem ceremony is a ritual that encodes psychological safety. The SDO's rituals must reinforce the behaviors that the culture is trying to build, and must be protected from being cancelled when the calendar gets tight.</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card-body">
<div class="culture-card-q">Rituals to establish and protect</div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Synchronized Sprint Reviews.</strong> All teams demo on the same day every sprint. Not a status meeting — a showcase. Cross-division attendance is expected from leadership. Builds shared awareness of what the office is delivering.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Quarterly PI Planning.</strong> Office-wide alignment on cross-division dependencies and goals every ~10 weeks. Creates a shared sense of planning horizon and mutual accountability across division lines.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Tradecraft Sessions.</strong> Monthly office-wide learning sessions where engineers share deep technical or domain expertise. Not project updates — genuine knowledge transfer. Reinforces that learning is a job responsibility.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Blameless Postmortem Reviews.</strong> Every significant incident results in a written postmortem with a mandatory review meeting. The review is a ritual — not a punishment. Attendance by at least one director signals leadership takes learning seriously.</div></div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card" style="--cc:var(--amber);">
<div class="culture-card-head">
<div class="culture-card-num">Lever 04 — Decisions</div>
<div class="culture-card-title">What the Organization<br>Chooses When It's Hard</div>
<div class="culture-card-def">Decisions are the most powerful cultural signal — and the most dangerous when they contradict stated values. Every time a trade-off is forced between mission and convenience, between quality and speed, between the right hire and the available hire — the decision reveals what the organization actually believes. One bad decision made visibly and without explanation can undo months of cultural investment. Decisions must be explained, especially when they are hard.</div>
</div>
<div class="culture-card-body">
<div class="culture-card-q">Decisions that will define this office's culture</div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Holding the bar on expertise in leadership.</strong> Refusing to fill a director role with a capable generalist when a domain expert is not yet ready — and communicating why — establishes that the experts-lead-experts principle is real.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Treating sunset milestones as hard commitments.</strong> When the first domain team is dissolved on schedule — even under pressure to keep it running — the organization learns that the modernization mandate has teeth.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Delaying a release for quality or security.</strong> When leadership chooses to delay a delivery rather than ship something that violates a security or quality standard, engineers internalize that "build to endure" is not aspirational.</div></div>
<div class="culture-example"><div class="culture-ex-dot"></div><div class="culture-ex-text"><strong>Protecting enabling team capacity.</strong> When UX &amp; Enablement facilitation time is defended against being cannibalized by delivery pressure, engineers learn that capability building is a first-class investment, not a luxury.</div></div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="page" id="page-openqs">
<div class="pg-eye">08 — Open Questions</div>
<div class="pg-title">Unresolved Design<br>Questions</div>
<p class="pg-desc">These are the known unknowns in this org design — areas where the structure has been deliberately left open because the answer requires input, policy decisions, or operational experience that does not yet exist. Each question is scoped, not vague. They need owners and resolution timelines before this design is considered final.</p>
<div class="oq-intro">
<strong>How to use this page:</strong> Each open question has a status (Open or Partially Resolved), a set of specific sub-questions that need answering, and a set of design considerations that will likely shape the answer. These should be assigned to owners and revisited at the first PI Planning session. Questions that remain open at 90 days should be escalated to the Director.
</div>
<div class="oq-sections">
<div class="oq-card" style="--oq-c:var(--violet);--oq-bg:var(--violet-bg);">
<div class="oq-head">
<div class="oq-head-l">
<div class="oq-title">Interaction with Platform Engineering</div>
<div class="oq-subtitle">The external Platform &amp; Enablement office provides CI/CD, infrastructure, DevSecOps, QA frameworks, and enterprise architecture. This design defines the interaction mode as X-as-a-Service, but the operational details of that relationship are unresolved.</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-status oq-open">Open</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-body">
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Questions to Resolve</div>
<div class="oq-q-list">
<div class="oq-q">Who is the named relationship owner between the SDO and the Platform &amp; Enablement office — is it the Technical Director, or is each division director responsible for their own platform relationship?</div>
<div class="oq-q">What is the SLA commitment from Platform &amp; Enablement to SDO divisions, and what is the escalation path when those SLAs are breached?</div>
<div class="oq-q">How are onboarding requests for new applications handled — is there a self-service model, or does every new SDO application require a formal intake with Platform Engineering?</div>
<div class="oq-q">How are cross-org architectural decisions made when Platform Engineering's standards conflict with an SDO division's technical direction? Who has final say?</div>
<div class="oq-q">Does the AI Engineering division have a separate relationship with Platform Engineering for GPU/compute infrastructure, or does it flow through the same channel as other divisions?</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Design Considerations</div>
<div class="oq-consideration">
<div class="oq-con">The X-as-a-Service model only works if Platform Engineering's interface is genuinely self-service. If SDO teams routinely need to collaborate with Platform Engineering to do routine work, the boundary has failed and a Collaboration mode should be formalized instead.</div>
<div class="oq-con">A single Technical Director relationship owner is likely the right model — it prevents five division directors from developing inconsistent expectations and allows the SDO to negotiate as a unified consumer.</div>
<div class="oq-con">The Datalytics division may have distinct infrastructure needs (data pipelines, warehouse compute, orchestration tooling) that don't fit the standard Platform Engineering model — this should be surfaced early.</div>
<div class="oq-con">Consider a quarterly joint review between SDO Technical Director and Platform Engineering leadership to surface friction, review SLA performance, and align on roadmap dependencies.</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-card" style="--oq-c:var(--amber);--oq-bg:var(--amber-bg);">
<div class="oq-head">
<div class="oq-head-l">
<div class="oq-title">IT Operations &amp; 24/7 Support</div>
<div class="oq-subtitle">The SDO owns mission-critical operational systems that may require round-the-clock availability. This design does not currently include an IT operations or 24/7 on-call function. How that need is met — and by whom — is unresolved.</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-status oq-open">Open</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-body">
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Questions to Resolve</div>
<div class="oq-q-list">
<div class="oq-q">Which systems in the SDO portfolio require 24/7 on-call coverage, and what is the criticality classification that triggers that requirement?</div>
<div class="oq-q">Is 24/7 on-call the responsibility of the engineering team that built and owns the system (you-build-it-you-run-it), or does a separate IT operations function exist for tier-1 triage?</div>
<div class="oq-q">How does after-hours incident escalation work — what is the paging model, and who is the first engineer paged for a P1 incident on a mission-critical system?</div>
<div class="oq-q">Does User Support (within UX &amp; Enablement) cover after-hours user-facing issues, or is that out of scope for the SDO and handled by a separate IT help desk?</div>
<div class="oq-q">Is there an IT Operations organization external to the SDO that provides infrastructure and NOC-level coverage, and how does the SDO hand off to them?</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Design Considerations</div>
<div class="oq-consideration">
<div class="oq-con">The "you-build-it-you-run-it" model (DevOps) is consistent with the engineering principles in this design and should be the default — each stream-aligned team in MAD and Sustain &amp; Sunset maintains an on-call rotation for systems they own.</div>
<div class="oq-con">A tiered model is likely needed: L1 triage by an IT operations function, L2 escalation to the on-call engineer in the owning team, L3 escalation to a senior engineer or director. The SDO should define L2+ and coordinate with whatever organization owns L1.</div>
<div class="oq-con">THEMIS, as a governance platform, may have different availability expectations than operational applications — its criticality classification should be established before go-live, not after.</div>
<div class="oq-con">If the ~50/50 FTE/contractor mix affects on-call eligibility (contractors may not be permitted to be on-call for certain system classifications), this constrains team staffing models and needs to be addressed in workforce planning.</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-card" style="--oq-c:var(--orange);--oq-bg:var(--orange-bg);">
<div class="oq-head">
<div class="oq-head-l">
<div class="oq-title">Forward Deployed Field Engineers</div>
<div class="oq-subtitle">Field engineers who operate forward — embedded with operational or mission partners outside the SDO's primary location — are not accounted for in the current org design. Their reporting chain, engagement model, and relationship to development teams is an open question.</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-status oq-open">Open</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-body">
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Questions to Resolve</div>
<div class="oq-q-list">
<div class="oq-q">Do forward deployed field engineers report into the SDO org at all, or are they organizationally owned by the operational or mission partner they are embedded with?</div>
<div class="oq-q">If they report into the SDO: which division owns them? Are they part of MAD (as embedded delivery engineers), Datalytics (as field data collection/integration), or a separate function?</div>
<div class="oq-q">How do field engineers feed requirements, bug reports, and operational feedback back to the development teams that own the systems they are supporting?</div>
<div class="oq-q">What is the career path for a field engineer — is it the same IC track as a headquarters engineer, or is there a distinct field engineering role with different expectations?</div>
<div class="oq-q">How are field engineers included in the synchronized sprint cadence — do they participate in sprint ceremonies remotely, or is their cadence independent?</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="oq-col">
<div class="oq-col-hd">Design Considerations</div>
<div class="oq-consideration">
<div class="oq-con">Forward deployment is most naturally a MAD responsibility if field engineers are doing application delivery or configuration work — they are stream-aligned to a mission-critical ops system and happen to be deployed near it. This is consistent with Team Topologies SA model.</div>
<div class="oq-con">If field engineers are primarily doing data collection, enrichment, or integration work — bridging field systems to SDO data pipelines — they may belong under Datalytics rather than MAD.</div>
<div class="oq-con">A matrix model may be required: field engineers report administratively to the SDO division that owns their primary work, but operate day-to-day under the authority of the field commander or mission partner. The SDO must define what "reporting to the SDO" means operationally when the engineer is not physically present.</div>
<div class="oq-con">Including field engineers in the shared sprint cadence — even partially — maintains engineering culture continuity and prevents them from developing a separate operational identity disconnected from the product teams they support.</div>
</div>
