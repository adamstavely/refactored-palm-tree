# AI Is Infrastructure. So What Do We Build?

*A response to John Gruber’s “AI Is Technology, Not a Product”*

-----

John Gruber published a piece this week that deserves more attention than it will probably get. Writing about Apple and the hype surrounding “killer AI products,” he makes a straightforward but clarifying claim: AI is technology, not a product. Not even a feature. It is substrate — pervasive infrastructure, the way wireless networking is infrastructure. You do not buy a Wi-Fi device. You buy a device that happens to use Wi-Fi to do things you actually care about.

He is right. And if he is right, the implications for how organizations think about AI strategy are far more disruptive than most leadership teams are currently prepared to admit.

-----

## The Wrong Question Is Dominating the Conversation

Most organizations, including most software development offices I encounter, are still asking a product question: *What AI thing should we build?* Or worse, the procurement version of that question: *What AI tool should we buy?* Both framings treat AI as a discrete deliverable — something you can ship, purchase, or demo to a steering committee. Both framings are wrong.

Gruber’s wireless networking analogy is instructive here. When Wi-Fi became ubiquitous, organizations did not convene working groups to identify “a killer wireless product.” They redesigned how work happened — remote access, mobile workflows, distributed collaboration — because the underlying capability changed what was possible. The technology did not arrive as a product to adopt. It arrived as a condition to adapt to.

AI is arriving the same way. Not as one thing, but as a property that will increasingly characterize every serious technical system an organization runs.

-----

## What Infrastructure Thinking Actually Demands

If you accept that AI is infrastructure, the strategic question shifts from *what do we build* to *how do we redesign work around a new substrate*. That is a fundamentally harder question. It requires a different kind of organizational courage.

Infrastructure thinking demands that you stop treating AI as the domain of a single team, a single office, or a single initiative. It demands that you examine every workflow, every interface, every human role in your delivery pipeline and ask honestly: how does this change when AI is load-bearing? Not bolted on. Not layered over. Load-bearing.

For software delivery organizations, this hits close to home. The question is not whether to add AI code assistance to your developer toolchain. The question is what software development looks like when AI can draft, test, review, and refactor at scale — and what that means for how you structure teams, how you define quality, and how you build the judgment capacity in your engineers to work effectively alongside systems they did not fully author.

-----

## The Trust Problem Is Not a Product Problem Either

Here is where Gruber’s framing opens a door that I want to walk through explicitly, because it connects directly to how I think about human-machine teaming.

If AI is infrastructure, then trust in AI systems is not a product feature. It is an organizational capability. You cannot ship trust. You cannot configure it in a settings panel. Trust between humans and AI systems is earned through consistent behavior, calibrated over time, through real work in real contexts.

Most organizations are not building for that. They are building for demonstration. They are optimizing for the moment when the system works impressively in a controlled environment, not for the sustained, day-to-day reliability that earns genuine reliance from the people doing the work.

The designers and engineers building AI-assisted systems have a responsibility here that goes beyond interface quality. If AI is infrastructure, then designing for human-AI teaming means designing for the long game — for how trust accumulates or erodes across hundreds of interactions, not just how a feature lands in a user acceptance test.

-----

## The Organizational Implication

Gruber closes with an observation worth sitting with: there is not going to be one killer AI device. Everything is going to be an AI device to some extent, just as everything today is a wireless device to some extent.

The parallel for organizations is this: there is not going to be one AI initiative. Every team, every product, every internal process is going to have some degree of AI embedded in it. The organizations that thrive will be the ones that treated AI as infrastructure from the beginning — and built the people, practices, and design principles to work with it deliberately.

That is not a technology strategy. That is an organizational design problem. And organizational design is something humans still have to do themselves.
