      <div class="label">01 / The Insight</div>
      <h2>The genius of LEGO is not the builder. It is the stud.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="body reveal">There is one interface, the stud and tube coupling, and it is held to a tolerance of a few microns. That single constraint is why every brick made since 1958 still snaps to every brick made today. A child builds a castle in an afternoon, not despite the standard but because of it.</p>
    <p class="body reveal soft">Notice what the child never does. They never machine a brick. They never read a compliance document. They never check whether two pieces will fit. The standard is invisible at the point of use because it lives in the part itself. Compliance is inherited the instant you pick up the piece.</p>
    <p class="body reveal">This is the whole thesis. Most organizations express standards as policy: a document, a checklist, a review gate. Policy is friction, it is optional under deadline pressure, and it is the first thing dropped when the business needs speed. A brick is none of those things. You cannot use it noncompliantly because the compliance is the shape of the object.</p>

    <p class="body reveal">There is a cost hidden in that tolerance. The reason the system is trusted is that the brick is excellent. A brick that almost fits would destroy the whole model, because builders would go back to checking every connection by hand. The lesson for us is blunt: you do not earn default adoption by mandate. You earn it by making the component good enough that rolling your own feels like a step backward.</p>

    <div class="callout reveal">
      <p>Put the standard inside the component, and adoption stops being a trade-off.</p>

  <!-- 05 GOVERNANCE BY CONSTRUCTION -->
  <section>
    <div class="sec-head reveal">
      <div class="label">05 / The Mechanism</div>
      <h2>Govern by construction, not by inspection.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="body reveal">There are two ways to make a standard real. You can inspect for it after the fact, with a review board, a checklist, and a gate that work has to pass through. Or you can build it into the thing teams already use, so the compliant outcome is the default one.</p>
    <p class="body reveal soft">Inspection scales badly. It adds a queue, it depends on the reviewer catching the problem, and it arrives too late to be cheap to fix. Construction scales by reuse. The logging SDK already emits the required fields. The pipeline template already runs the required scans. The infrastructure module is already encrypted and tagged. Nobody had to remember, because remembering was never the mechanism.</p>
    <div class="twocol stagger">
      <div class="panel warm">
        <div class="ctag">Inspection &middot; the gate</div>
        <h3>Standards as something you check</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>A review board approves work before it ships.</li>
          <li>Compliance depends on a human noticing.</li>
          <li>Adds a queue and a bottleneck.</li>
          <li>Found late, which is when fixes are expensive.</li>
          <li>The business experiences it purely as delay.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="panel cool">
        <div class="ctag">Construction &middot; the brick</div>
        <h3>Standards as something you inherit</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>The component arrives compliant by default.</li>
          <li>The wrong thing is harder to do than the right thing.</li>
          <li>Scales with reuse, not headcount.</li>
          <li>Correct from the first commit.</li>
          <li>The business experiences it as speed.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>
    <p class="body reveal" style="margin-top:26px">Keep the gate, but shrink it to the exception path. Standard work composed from bricks ships freely. Genuinely novel work, the thing no brick covers yet, is where a human review still earns its cost, and it is also your backlog of bricks to build next.</p>
  </section>

  <!-- 06 BUSINESS CASE -->
  <section>
    <div class="sec-head reveal">
      <div class="label">06 / The Business Case</div>
      <h2>Standards are not a tax on speed. The lack of them is.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="lead-in reveal">The business believes standards slow delivery. That belief is correct about standards as documents and exactly backward about standards as components. Here is the real ledger.</p>
    <div class="twocol stagger">
      <div class="panel warm">
        <div class="ctag">Ad hoc &middot; the hidden cost</div>
        <h3>Every team re-machines the same bricks</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>Re-solve auth, logging, and deploy on every project.</li>
          <li>The cost does not disappear, it moves to later.</li>
          <li>It resurfaces as rework, integration failures, and incidents.</li>
          <li>New hires face a different setup on every team.</li>
          <li>Speed today is borrowed against maintenance tomorrow.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="panel cool">
        <div class="ctag">Reusable components &middot; the subsidy</div>
        <h3>Snap together what already works</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>Ship faster because nothing is built twice.</li>
          <li>Security and observability arrive baked in.</li>
          <li>An engineer can move teams because the bricks are the same.</li>
          <li>Swap one piece without rebuilding the model.</li>
          <li>The cost is paid once and amortized across every build.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>
    <p class="bigline reveal">Standards as documents are a tax. Standards as <span class="y">components</span> are a subsidy.</p>
    <p class="ask reveal">So do not ask the business to value standards in the abstract. Ask it to <b>fund the brick factory</b> and to measure delivery speed before and after. The argument is not philosophical, it is a lead time number.</p>
  </section>

  <!-- 07 ADOPTION -->
  <section>
    <div class="sec-head reveal">
      <div class="label">07 / The Operating Model</div>
      <h2>Make the compliant path the easy path.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="lead-in reveal">A brick nobody reaches for is shelfware that also slowed you down to build. Adoption is the product. These are the levers that earn it, in rough order of impact.</p>
    <div class="steps stagger">
      <div class="step"><div class="n">1</div><div><h4>Win on defaults</h4><p>The single strongest lever. The path of least resistance must also be the compliant one. If the starter template, the generated service, and the documented example all already use the brick, most teams never make an active choice at all.</p></div></div>
      <div class="step"><div class="n">2</div><div><h4>Ship the set, not just the brick</h4><p>Do not hand teams a pile of parts and a spec. Give them an assembled reference, the golden path, that runs end to end out of the box. People copy a working example far more readily than they assemble from primitives.</p></div></div>
      <div class="step"><div class="n">3</div><div><h4>Treat docs and support as product surface</h4><p>The brick is only as good as the ten minutes it takes to start using it. Quickstart, clear examples, a real owner who answers questions. A brilliant component with no on ramp loses to a worse one that is easy to start.</p></div></div>
      <div class="step"><div class="n">4</div><div><h4>Run an intake, take requests seriously</h4><p>The factory has customers, so it needs a way to hear them. When a team wants a brick that does not exist, that is roadmap signal, not a nuisance. The bricks people pull are the ones that spread on their own.</p></div></div>
      <div class="step"><div class="n">5</div><div><h4>Make migration off the old way real</h4><p>Adoption stalls when leaving the legacy pattern is painful. Provide codemods, adapters, and a clear deprecation window. You are not just offering a better brick, you are paying down the cost of switching to it.</p></div></div>
    </div>
    <p class="body reveal" style="margin-top:26px">Notice the word that does not appear in that list: mandate. Mandate a brick before it is good and teams either build quiet workarounds or comply maliciously, hitting the letter and missing the point. Earn the default first. Mandate, if ever, only the few seams where divergence genuinely cannot be tolerated.</p>
  </section>

  <!-- 08 EVOLUTION -->
  <section>
    <div class="sec-head reveal">
      <div class="label">08 / Evolution</div>
      <h2>Change the system without breaking the system.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="body reveal">A fair objection: software standards are not frozen like a plastic brick, they have to change. True, and LEGO is still the right model. The catalog has grown by thousands of new pieces over decades, while the stud spec at the center barely moved. New capability lives in new parts. The interface stays stable.</p>
    <p class="body reveal soft">Translate that into three habits. First, version the bricks and let teams adopt new versions on their own schedule, never with a flag day. Second, hold backward compatibility at the interface as close to sacred as you can, and when you must break it, ship the migration path in the same release. Third, deprecate out loud and on a clock, with a stated window and tooling to move, so nobody is stranded on a part you stopped supporting.</p>
    <div class="callout reveal">
      <p>Add new pieces freely. Change the coupling almost never, and never without a path forward.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <div class="sec-head reveal">
      <div class="label">09 / The Pressure Test</div>
      <h2>The ways this goes wrong.</h2>
    </div>
    <p class="lead-in reveal">The model is simple to state and easy to corrupt. Watch for these, because each one is common and each one quietly kills adoption.</p>
    <div class="tests stagger">
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>The ivory tower</h3><p>The factory designs bricks in isolation and is surprised nobody uses them. Fix it by treating engineers as customers and building only what is pulled.</p></div>
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>The mandate by memo</h3><p>A policy declares the brick required before it is good. Teams route around it or comply without buying in. Earn the default, do not decree it.</p></div>
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>The one off piece</h3><p>A brick fits exactly one team and nothing else, so it never compounds. Build for composition across many products, not for a single moment.</p></div>
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>The leaky abstraction</h3><p>The brick hides complexity until it does not, then the team has to understand both the brick and what it wrapped. Keep escape hatches honest and the seams clean.</p></div>
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>The gold plated brick</h3><p>The factory over builds for cases nobody has, and the part is slow, heavy, and hard to learn. Ship the smallest brick that solves the real, repeated problem.</p></div>
      <div class="test"><span class="bad">Anti pattern</span><h3>Guardrails become gates</h3><p>What started as a default hardens into a blocking checkpoint, and the friction returns. A guardrail keeps you safe at speed. A gate stops you. Keep choosing the guardrail.</p></div>
    </div>
