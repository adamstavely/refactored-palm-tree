<div class="pg-eye">07 — Technical Talent</div>
<div class="pg-title">Technical Staff Structure<br>&amp; Career Framework</div>
<p class="pg-desc">The office organizes technical talent on a single principle: experts lead experts. From entry-level engineers to the Technical Director, every leadership role is held by someone with deep expertise in the domain they lead. This is not a preference — it is a structural requirement that shapes how the office hires, promotes, and evaluates its people.</p>
<div class="talent-philosophy">
<div class="talent-philosophy-hd">The Experts-Lead-Experts Paradigm</div>
<p>Conventional organizations separate management from expertise. As companies grow, they tend to install general managers above technical teams — people who can coordinate but cannot evaluate, judge, or improve the work beneath them. The result is predictable: technical decisions get slower, quality declines, and the best individual contributors leave because they can't learn from their managers.</p>
<p>This office takes a different position, one validated by Apple's functional organization model: the assumption is that it is easier to train an expert to manage well than to train a general manager to develop genuine technical expertise. Engineering leaders at every level — team lead, division director, deputy director, technical director — must be capable of engaging substantively with the work their teams produce. They must be able to push, probe, and evaluate decisions, not just track them. Leaders are chosen for domain mastery first; management capability is developed from that foundation.</p>
<p>Three behaviors define what experts-lead-experts looks like in practice. All three are expected of every engineering leader in this office, calibrated to their level and span of responsibility.</p>
<div class="exp-trio">
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Deep Expertise</div>
<p>Leaders possess genuine expertise in the domain they lead and can engage meaningfully with technical work at multiple levels of detail. They are recognized as authoritative within their specialty — not merely senior in title. Engineers choose this org because they can learn from the people above them.</p>
</div>
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Immersion in Details</div>
<p>Leaders know the details of their organization at least three levels deep. This is essential for effective cross-functional decision-making. Leaders who cannot engage with the details of their domain cannot evaluate trade-offs, detect failure, or earn the trust of their engineers.</p>
</div>
<div class="exp-card">
<div class="exp-card-hd">Collaborative Debate</div>
<p>Leaders hold strong, well-grounded views and advocate for them — and are willing to change their minds when presented with better evidence. Effective functional organizations require leaders who can be simultaneously partisan and open-minded, separating how right a decision is from how hard it is to execute.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="pg-sub-eye" style="margin-bottom:12px;">Discretionary Leadership Model</div>
<div class="talent-philosophy" style="margin-bottom:28px;">
<p>As engineering leaders take on larger spans and broader responsibilities, they cannot own everything at the same level of detail. The Discretionary Leadership Model, adapted from Apple University, defines four modes of engagement that leaders use to allocate their attention across the portfolio of work they are responsible for.</p>
<div class="dlm-grid">
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-own">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">High Expertise · Deep Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Owning</div>
<p>Areas where the leader has deep expertise and is highly involved in the details. The leader sets direction, evaluates the work, and is accountable for the outcome. This is the core of what it means to be an expert leading experts.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-lrn">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">Low Expertise · Deep Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Learning</div>
<p>New domains the leader must grow into. Requires a genuine beginner's mindset — asking questions without pretending to have answers. Only critical new responsibilities should be in this quadrant; it is expensive and time-consuming.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-tch">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">High Expertise · Lower Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Teaching</div>
<p>Areas of deep expertise where the leader has developed others sufficiently to reduce direct involvement. The leader provides critique, feedback, and standards rather than owning the decisions. Requires experts to teach — general managers cannot do this.</p>
</div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-del">
<div class="dlm-quad-tag">Lower Expertise · Lower Involvement</div>
<div class="dlm-quad-hd">Delegating</div>
<p>Areas where others have the requisite capability and the leader sets objectives, monitors progress, and holds teams accountable. This is closer to general management. Leaders in this office should spend the minority of their time here.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="pg-sub-eye" style="margin-bottom:16px;">Career Ladder — Dual Track (ENG-FW-2026)</div>

<strong>Track Decision at GS-13 (Level 4).</strong> The Individual Contributor and Management tracks are equally valued and both lead to executive-level impact. Engineers choose at GS-13 based on where they generate more energy and create more leverage — technical depth or people leadership. Track switches are possible; many senior leaders have held roles in both. Promotions to GS-13 and above are <strong>competitive and based on organizational need</strong>, not individual readiness alone.

<div class="pg-eye">08 — Theory of Change</div>
<div class="pg-title">Theory of Change</div>
<p class="pg-desc">A theory of change is an explicit, testable model of how and why a set of interventions will produce desired outcomes. It is the logic that connects what we do to what we want to see happen — and what must be true for that connection to hold. Without one, reorganizations are structural changes in search of a rationale.</p>

<div class="talent-philosophy" style="margin-bottom:24px;">
<div class="talent-philosophy-hd">What Is a Theory of Change?</div>
<p>A theory of change is not a plan. A plan describes what you will do. A theory of change explains why doing those things should produce the outcomes you want — and under what conditions. It makes causal assumptions explicit so they can be tested, challenged, and updated.</p>
<p>A well-formed theory of change has five components: the <strong style="color:var(--text);">problem</strong> being addressed, the <strong style="color:var(--text);">inputs</strong> committed to solving it, the <strong style="color:var(--text);">activities</strong> those inputs fund, the <strong style="color:var(--text);">outputs</strong> those activities produce, and the <strong style="color:var(--text);">outcomes</strong> those outputs cause. Between each link is an assumption — something that must be true for the chain to hold. Identifying those assumptions is the most valuable part of the exercise, because they reveal the conditions under which the theory breaks down.</p>
<p>For this reorg, the theory of change answers: if we redesign the Software Development Office this way, what do we expect to change, how, and why? It is a hypothesis, not a guarantee — and treating it as a hypothesis means we build in the means to test it.</p>
<div class="tt-principles" style="margin-top:20px;">
<div class="tt-principle"><div class="tt-principle-hd">Problem</div><p>What condition we are trying to change. The baseline state that is unacceptable and the gap between it and the desired state.</p></div>
<div class="tt-principle"><div class="tt-principle-hd">Inputs → Activities → Outputs</div><p>Resources committed, actions taken, and the direct products of those actions. These are within our control.</p></div>
<div class="tt-principle"><div class="tt-principle-hd">Outcomes → Impact</div><p>The changes in behavior, capability, or performance that outputs cause. These are the goals — and they require assumptions to hold.</p></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="pg-eye">09 — Measurement</div>
<div class="pg-title">OKRs, KPIs &amp;<br>Alignment Framework</div>
<p class="pg-desc">Strategy without measurement is aspiration. This page defines how the SDO translates vision and mission into measurable commitments — and how OKRs and KPIs stack from office-level intent down to team-level metrics, creating a coherent line of sight from sprint output to mission impact.</p>

<div class="talent-philosophy" style="margin-bottom:24px;">
<div class="talent-philosophy-hd">The Alignment Stack</div>
<p>Every metric in this office should be traceable upward to a strategic objective, and every strategic objective should be traceable upward to the mission. The alignment stack has four layers. <strong style="color:var(--text);">Vision</strong> is the long-term picture of what the SDO becomes. <strong style="color:var(--text);">Mission</strong> is the standing purpose that defines why the office exists. <strong style="color:var(--text);">Strategy</strong> is the set of choices about where to focus to move from current state toward vision. <strong style="color:var(--text);">OKRs</strong> are time-bound commitments to measurable progress against the strategy. <strong style="color:var(--text);">KPIs</strong> are the continuous health indicators that tell us whether our systems are working — regardless of any specific objective cycle.</p>
<div class="dlm-grid" style="margin-top:20px;">
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-own"><div class="dlm-quad-tag">Directional · Stable</div><div class="dlm-quad-hd">Vision + Mission</div><p>Why the office exists and what it is becoming. Changes rarely. Provides the fixed north star that all objectives navigate toward.</p></div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-lrn"><div class="dlm-quad-tag">Annual · Adaptive</div><div class="dlm-quad-hd">Strategy</div><p>The set of choices that define where the office bets its attention and resources this year. Should be explicit enough to say no to some things.</p></div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-tch"><div class="dlm-quad-tag">Quarterly · Committed</div><div class="dlm-quad-hd">OKRs</div><p>Objectives (what we want to achieve) and Key Results (how we will know we achieved it). Ambitious but achievable. 70% attainment is success at stretch targets.</p></div>
<div class="dlm-quad dlm-del"><div class="dlm-quad-tag">Continuous · Leading</div><div class="dlm-quad-hd">KPIs</div><p>Always-on health indicators that surface trends before they become problems. Not tied to a specific objective cycle — they run continuously and inform OKR setting.</p></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="pg-sub-eye" style="margin-bottom:16px;">OKRs vs KPIs — The Critical Distinction</div>
<div style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:1fr 1fr;gap:14px;margin-bottom:24px;">
<div class="mvv-card mvv-mission" style="border-top-color:var(--blue);">
<div class="mvv-eye" style="color:var(--blue);">Objectives & Key Results (OKRs)</div>
<div class="mvv-statement" style="font-size:13px;">Time-bounded commitments to change something specific.</div>
<div class="mvv-sub">OKRs answer: <em>what are we trying to accomplish this quarter, and how will we know we succeeded?</em> They should be ambitious — if you're hitting 100% of your key results every quarter, your targets aren't stretching you. They are qualitative at the objective level (inspiring direction) and quantitative at the key result level (measurable outcomes). OKRs expire. They are set, pursued, graded, and retired.</div>
</div>
<div class="mvv-card mvv-vision" style="border-top-color:var(--teal);">
<div class="mvv-eye" style="color:var(--teal);">Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)</div>
<div class="mvv-statement" style="font-size:13px;">Continuous health signals that never expire.</div>
<div class="mvv-sub">KPIs answer: <em>is the system working the way we designed it to work?</em> They are leading and lagging indicators of organizational health that run continuously — not tied to a quarter or an objective. A KPI going red doesn't mean you failed an OKR; it means something in the system needs attention. Good KPIs make problems visible early enough to act on them.</div>
</div>
</div>
