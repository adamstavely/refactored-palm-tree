# Execute Has Two Meanings

*And most organizations are only comfortable with one of them.*

-----

I said it off the cuff on an internal podcast. Someone asked me how I think about technical direction and I said, without much preamble: “Execute has two meanings. You can execute a project to completion, or you can execute it because it needs to die.”

Apparently that landed.

I’ve been thinking about why ever since. Not because the wordplay is clever — it’s not particularly — but because the reaction revealed something real about how most organizations think about “delivery.” We treat execution as synonymous with completion. We measure it that way. We reward it that way. We almost never pause to ask whether the thing we’re executing deserves to finish.

That asymmetry is expensive.

-----

## The Comfortable Definition

When most leaders say “we need to execute,” they mean get it done. Ship it. Cross the finish line. There is nothing wrong with that instinct. Completion is genuinely hard, and organizations that cannot finish things are in serious trouble.

But the fetishization of completion creates a quiet pathology. It turns sunk cost into moral obligation. It frames continuation as discipline and termination as failure. It makes people afraid to raise their hand and say what everyone in the room already suspects: this thing is not working, and more time will not fix it.

The sunk cost fallacy does not need an introduction. Everyone has heard of it. Almost no one is immune to it in practice, because in practice it is not a cognitive error — it is a social and political one. Killing a project means admitting that the original decision was wrong. It means someone loses a budget, a headcount, a roadmap item they fought for. It means standing in front of leadership and saying we got this one wrong, and here is what we learned, and here is what we are doing instead.

That is genuinely hard. It requires a kind of institutional courage that most organizations do not explicitly develop or reward.

-----

## What “Execute” Actually Demands

True technical direction — the kind that earns trust over time — requires comfort with both meanings.

The directive to execute a project to completion is a bet. You are betting that the problem is real, that the approach is sound, that the value is achievable, and that the cost of continuing is worth it relative to everything else you could be doing. Good leaders revisit that bet continuously. They do not just ask “are we on track?” They ask “should we still be on this track at all?”

The decision to execute a platform or program that is not working is not failure. It is accuracy. It is the organization becoming more honest about what it actually has. And in most organizations with any real technical complexity, there are legacy systems, partially-built capabilities, and long-running programs consuming significant resources that, if evaluated freshly today, would not survive the scrutiny.

The question is whether you have the clarity and the standing to say so.

-----

## The Decommission Problem

Decommissioning is unglamorous work. Nobody puts it on a highlight reel. There is no ribbon-cutting ceremony for a platform you shut down, no all-hands slide that celebrates the team that finally killed a system that was dragging the organization sideways for three years.

But the absence of decommissioning is visible in every engineering organization that has been around long enough. It shows up as sprawl: duplicate capabilities, fragmented tooling, tribal knowledge locked in systems that no longer receive investment but somehow still cannot be turned off. It shows up as drag: the ongoing cost in time, attention, and operational overhead of maintaining things that deliver no forward value. It shows up as learned helplessness: engineers who have stopped asking why something works the way it does because they have made peace with the fact that it is not really supposed to.

None of that is inevitable. It is the accumulated result of defaulting to the comfortable definition of execute, over and over, until continuity becomes inertia.

Decommissioning requires a different kind of rigor than delivery. You have to actually understand what the system does, what depends on it, and what would replace it. You have to sequence the transition carefully, because the last thing you want is to trade a dragging legacy system for an outage. And you have to manage the human side of it — the people who built it, the stakeholders who depend on it, the institutional memory it carries.

That is real technical direction. It is harder than it looks from the outside. And it is worth doing.

-----

## Deliver Platforms That Do

The flip side of the coin is where the energy goes once you have freed it from what was not working.

Execution as delivery — the kind that actually compounds — requires the same honest assessment. Not every new thing deserves to finish either. But when you have made a clear-eyed decision that something is worth building, and you have cleared the runway by not dragging legacy weight you should have cut earlier, delivery changes character. It becomes faster. It becomes cleaner. The team knows what they are building and why, and they are not simultaneously babysitting the system they were supposed to replace three years ago.

There is a version of this that sounds like obvious management advice: stop doing things that are not working, do more of what is. The obvious version is not wrong, it is just incomplete. The hard version is about organizational honesty — about building the culture and the processes and the leadership practices that make it safe to say “this is not working” before the situation becomes undeniable.

The organizations that are good at delivery are usually also good at stopping. That correlation is not accidental.

-----

## The Tagline That Earned It

After the podcast clip started making the rounds, someone put together a graphic with a tagline I genuinely wish I had written first:

*Decommission what holds you back. Deliver what moves you forward.*

That is the whole framework, right there. Two meanings. Both require courage. Both require clarity. Neither works without the other.

If your organization is only comfortable with one of them, that is worth sitting with.
