The Problem

AI adoption in legal practice is not a future event to be planned for. It is a present reality to be managed. Attorneys use AI-assisted research tools, large language models for drafting, and AI-summarized evidence review daily — with or without firm authorization, and in most firms, without the infrastructure to make it safe.

The specific failure modes are not theoretical. They have occurred at peer firms and been reported publicly.

Privilege Leakage

RAG-based tools have surfaced Matter A content in prompts scoped to Matter B. Without matter-scoped retrieval enforcement, this is structurally inevitable.

Fabricated Citations

Attorneys have filed briefs citing cases that do not exist. Several have faced bar discipline. AI models hallucinate citations with high fluency and apparent confidence.

Stale Evidence

Analysts have presented AI-generated summaries without knowing that the underlying sources had been superseded, contradicted, or subject to court-ordered reconsideration.

Uncalibrated Trust

Junior associates with limited ability to evaluate AI output quality are accepting AI conclusions at higher rates than senior attorneys — systematic quality asymmetry invisible to supervision.

The Urgent Need

Three converging pressures make this urgent rather than important.

Professional Liability

Bar discipline is already occurring

Fabricated citations, unsupported legal conclusions, and undisclosed AI contributions create malpractice exposure that has materialized at peer firms. A single significant matter where AI-generated content was relied upon without verification can exceed the multi-year cost of this platform in a single claim.

Regulatory Timing

Bar associations are moving now

Bar associations in multiple jurisdictions are actively developing AI competence and disclosure requirements. Firms that have PCES, PGS, and ERAS operational before disclosure requirements are mandated are compliant on day one. Firms building these capabilities after the mandate are in remediation mode under scrutiny.

Competitive Position

Clients are beginning to ask

Sophisticated clients — financial institutions, major corporates, government agencies — are beginning to ask how outside counsel governs AI use. A firm that cannot answer that question credibly is at a disadvantage in panel reviews and RFP processes against firms that can demonstrate governed AI posture.

  • Provision disappearance detection — Flags provisions absent in a later version without a corresponding replacement or deletion note

Source Type Badges — Design Rationale

Why source type is surfaced at claim level

LLMs produce uniformly fluent output regardless of how a claim was generated — whether retrieved from a specific document, inferred from training weights, synthesized across partial sources, or derived from an AI-generated transcript of a deposition. That uniformity is the problem. Fluency acts as a proxy for credibility in standard interfaces, and the source type of a claim has direct implications for what verification is even possible.

A claim marked GRND can be checked against a specific document. A claim marked PARAM cannot — the appropriate response is independent verification or explicit acknowledgment that the claim is unverifiable through normal analyst workflow. A claim marked TRANSCRIPT requires checking against the recording itself, not the document the transcript came from — because the transcript is a derivative artifact with its own error rate. These are qualitatively different epistemic tasks, and the interface must make the distinction visible before the analyst acts.

Text-derived badges (3)

Claims derived from text sources — retrieved documents, training weights, or synthesized from multiple partial matches. These are the original three source types from the initial design. TCS/MIMIR calibration models have the richest verification outcome history for these types.

GRND PARAM SYNTH

Media-derived badges (5)

Claims derived from AI analysis of media artifacts or from OCR extraction. These require separate TCS/MIMIR calibration streams because the cognitive task, failure mode taxonomy, and available verification actions differ materially from text-derived claims. MAS/EIDOLON authenticity risk scores are integrated for VIDEO, AUDIO, IMAGE, and TRANSCRIPT badges.

TRANSCRIPT VIDEO AUDIO IMAGE OCR

TRANSCRIPT is not GRND

An AI-generated transcript of a video deposition is not equivalent to a retrieved source document. The transcript has its own error rate — ASR (automatic speech recognition) accuracy varies by audio quality, speaker accent, technical terminology, and background noise. A claim sourced from a transcript inherits the transcript's accuracy profile, not the authority of the original recording. Showing TRANSCRIPT rather than GRND makes this derivation visible at the claim level. The verification action set reflects this: "Transcript may be inaccurate" routes to MAS/EIDOLON for transcript accuracy corpus update — a different signal from "Misrepresents source," which routes as a synthesis failure.

Confidence ceiling concept

Source type creates an upper bound on TCS/MIMIR confidence scores independent of the model's self-reported confidence. An OCR-derived claim from a region with 62% OCR confidence cannot carry a HIGH confidence badge regardless of how confidently the model stated it — the accuracy of the extracted text is the binding constraint. Similarly, a claim derived from a media artifact with a high MAS/EIDOLON authenticity risk score receives a reduced confidence ceiling: the claim may accurately represent what the artifact showed, but what the artifact showed may itself be fabricated. The badge system surfaces both the TCS confidence score and the source type — readers see both signals independently.
